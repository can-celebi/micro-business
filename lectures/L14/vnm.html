From ordinal to cardinal utility with vNM

Wieland (Choice theory) · 23.11.2026 · for Can to discuss, not a deck · Can's words (specs/Risk.md, Addition 1): "we want a super low level but still intuitive and covering all the general idea and details for how we move from ordinal to cardinal with vNM", then expected utility · class conventions: Frank's U(M) of total wealth; risk averse / risk neutral / risk seeking · the pictures move: drag the sliders · checked by Ulutin (Denetlemeci) before you see it
In short
  1. Until now, utility numbers only had to rank things. Any numbers going up in the same order did the job (ordinal).
  2. With lotteries, a ranking is not enough. Two sets of numbers with the same ranking can give opposite answers on "sure prize or lottery ticket?".
  3. We build the right numbers with one question: "at which chance of the best prize is the ticket exactly as good as the sure prize?" That chance is the utility of the prize: u(worst) = 0, u(best) = 1, u(prize) = p.
  4. With these numbers, every lottery turns into "some chance of the best prize". That chance is exactly the expected utility, so expected utility ranks lotteries.
  5. Four plain assumptions (the axioms) make this work. The numbers are now fixed up to a + b·u with b > 0, like Celsius and Fahrenheit. Other changes, such as squaring, are not allowed any more (cardinal).
One running example with three lottery prizes, 9 steps, a live picture in every step except the axiom list (step 6) and the summary card (step 8). Points for you to decide are at the end.

Step 1 · Three prizes, one ranking

A café lottery: everyone wins something. The prizes are a smartphone, headphones or a coffee.

Our student ranks them: smartphone better than headphones, headphones better than coffee. This is what we did in L06: a complete and transitive ranking.

Step 3 · Add a lottery: the ranking alone cannot answer

New offer: give back the headphones for a ticket that pays the smartphone with 50% and the coffee with 50%.

Try the obvious rule, the average of the ticket's numbers. With coffee 0 and smartphone 10 the ticket is worth 5. Drag the headphones' number: below 5 the rule says "take the ticket", above 5 it says "keep the headphones". Every position has the same ranking from step 2. So the ranking does not tell us the answer. We need numbers that also say how much better the headphones are than the coffee, compared with the smartphone.

Step 4 · The measuring question

Fix the ends: u(coffee) = 0 and u(smartphone) = 1. Then ask the student one question about the middle prize.

"Here are your headphones. Here is a ticket: smartphone with chance p, coffee otherwise. For which p are both exactly equally good for you?" At p = 0 the student keeps the headphones, at 100% the student takes the ticket, so somewhere in between the two are equally good. Our student says 60%. That number becomes the utility: u(headphones) = 0.6.

chance of the smartphone on the ticket, p:

We still only ask the student to compare things (rankings). The numbers come out of how the student ranks lotteries. That links to L06: we never measure happiness.

Step 5 · Using the numbers: every lottery is "a chance of the smartphone"

A new ticket: smartphone with 30%, headphones with 70%. Is it better than a ticket "smartphone 75%, coffee 25%"?

From step 4, the headphones are exactly as good as "smartphone 60%, coffee 40%". Swap them in. Then the first ticket gives the smartphone with 30% + 70% × 60% = 72%, and the coffee otherwise. Both tickets are now "smartphone or coffee". A higher chance of the smartphone is better (this also follows from the axioms, step 6), so the student takes ticket B: 75% beats 72%.

30% · 1 + 70% · 0.6 = 0.72   <   75% · 1 + 25% · 0 = 0.75

That sum, chance × utility added up, is the expected utility. It is exactly the total chance of the smartphone after the swap. That is why expected utility ranks lotteries. Drag the first ticket's chance of the smartphone to see the swap.

Step 6 · What the axioms buy (one plain sentence each)

Steps 1 to 5 used four assumptions about how the student ranks lotteries. These are the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms.

Complete · used in step 1
Any two lotteries can be compared.
Transitive · used in step 1
If A is at least as good as B, and B is at least as good as C, then A is at least as good as C.
Continuity · used in step 4
For a middle prize, some chance p of the best prize (worst prize otherwise) makes the ticket exactly as good as the middle prize.
Independence · used in step 5
Mixing two tickets with the same third ticket, at the same chance, keeps their order: if you prefer A to B, you prefer "A or the third ticket" to "B or the third ticket" (and equally good stays equally good).
  • Completeness and transitivity are the L06 rationality conditions, now for lotteries.
  • Continuity says no prize is infinitely better or worse than another. Without it, step 4 has no answer.
  • Independence does two jobs in step 5. The "equally good" case allows the swap of the headphones. The "prefer" case gives "a higher chance of the smartphone is better", so 75% beats 72%. Allais and Ellsberg attack this axiom next week.
  • Two names, two ideas: this is the independence axiom of expected utility (about mixing tickets). It is not Arrow's independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) from voting, which says the order of two options must not depend on a third option. Both come up next week, so they keep separate names.
  • Step 5 also uses one more assumption: only the final chances of each prize matter (30% + 70% × 60% = 72%), not how the ticket was built (in textbooks: reduction of compound lotteries). Many texts fold this into the definition of a lottery.

The result (von Neumann and Morgenstern, Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, Princeton University Press, 1944; proof in the appendix of the 2nd ed., 1947; the axioms here in today's textbook form): if the ranking of lotteries satisfies these four axioms, there are numbers u for the prizes such that a lottery is better exactly when its expected utility is higher. Those numbers are fixed up to a + b·u with b > 0 (step 7).

Step 7 · Why only "a + b·u" changes are allowed now

The 0 and the 1 at the ends were our choice, like choosing a scale for temperature.

Fahrenheit = 32 + 1.8 × Celsius. Hot stays hot, and "halfway between" stays halfway between. In the same way, any a + b·u with b > 0 keeps every lottery answer. A change like u² or √u keeps the ranking of the sure prizes but bends the scale, so some lottery answer flips. Click a change and watch the two comparisons.

Ordinal (before): any increasing change allowed, only the order means something. Cardinal (now): only a + b·u, so comparisons of differences mean something. Here coffee → headphones (0.6) is a bigger step than headphones → smartphone (0.4) under every allowed scale. This is what lets us talk about diminishing marginal utility next.

Step 8 · The whole worked example on one card

  1. Ends: u(coffee) = 0, u(smartphone) = 1.
  2. Question: headphones or "smartphone with p, coffee otherwise"? Equally good at p = 60%. So u(headphones) = 0.6.
  3. Ticket A: smartphone 30%, headphones 70%. EU = 0.3 · 1 + 0.7 · 0.6 = 0.72.
  4. Ticket B: smartphone 75%, coffee 25%. EU = 0.75 · 1 + 0.25 · 0 = 0.75.
  5. Prediction: the student takes ticket B, although B has a 25% chance of ending with only a coffee.
  6. Check: sure headphones (0.6) against "smartphone 50%, coffee 50%" (0.5): the student keeps the headphones.

Step 9 · Preview: the same question with money

Replace the prizes by final wealth M: worst €0, best €100. So U(0) = 0 and U(100) = 1.

Ask: "which sure amount is exactly as good as a 50/50 ticket on €0 or €100?" If the answer is €40, then U(40) = 0.5. That point lies above the straight line, so U bends: diminishing marginal utility and risk aversion. This leads to expected utility with U(M), concave/linear/convex and the certainty equivalent (Can's next bullets). Drag the sure amount.
