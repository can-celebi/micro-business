choice under uncertainty

Choice under uncertainty is choice over gambles.
A job: take the offer now, or wait for a better one?
A flat: sign now, or keep looking?
A flight: buy now, or hope it gets cheaper?
A gamble is a list of outcomes, each with a chance.
Today: what makes a gamble attractive, and what does it mean to prefer one gamble to another?

John von Neumann: stories

He worked on the atomic bomb at Los Alamos: he helped design the explosive lenses of the implosion bomb.
He played German marches at full volume on his office phonograph; the neighbours complained, Einstein included.

He once beat a world-famous expert on Byzantine history on a date. The expert later joked: he would come again “if Johnny promises not to discuss Byzantine history” (a friend told LIFE magazine, 1957).

Let's all agree: we strictly prefer S to H. S ≻ H.

Let's assume our preferences are complete and transitive.

continuity

Continuity: for any outcome between the best and the worst, there always exists a probability p such that you are indifferent between that outcome for sure and the lottery ticket (best with chance p, worst with chance 1 − p).
In our example: there is a p with H ∼ (S p, C 1 − p). We assumed p = 60%.
In words: every outcome can be written as a mix of the best outcome and the worst outcome.
Without continuity we could not give H a number between u(C) and u(S).

independence

You strictly prefer S to H: S ≻ H.
lottery ticket A
S with chance 50%
C with chance 50%
lottery ticket B
H with chance 50%
C with chance 50%
The grey half is identical in A and B. They differ only in the other half: S in A, H in B.
Independence: an identical part cannot change your choice. Since S ≻ H, you also strictly prefer A to B: (S 50%, C 50%) ≻ (H 50%, C 50%).

expected utility

Expected utility (EU): multiply the utility of each outcome by its probability and add up.
Written out: EU = p₁ · u(x₁) + p₂ · u(x₂) + … + pₙ · u(xₙ).
Shorter: EU = Σ pᵢ · u(xᵢ).
Σ is a capital Greek S, for 'sum': add up over all outcomes.
Read it as: for every outcome, multiply its probability by its utility; then add everything up.
Lottery 1 has two outcomes, so two terms: 0.4 · 0.6 + 0.6 · 0.
In the same way, EV = Σ pᵢ · xᵢ.

backup · risk attitudes

Compare the gamble with its expected wealth for sure.
Risk averse: you prefer the expected wealth for sure, for every risky gamble. That is a concave U.

FAQ (Carpenter fb29983bfc57, spoken lines):1. Why not just ask people how much they like risk?"We want to see choices. A choice says what you would give up for what; a word like 'careful' means something different to each of us."2. Why do we need more than one task?"Because the answers don't agree. A study gave 1,507 people six different methods, and their risk preferences were not consistent across methods. The authors suggest we may partly build our preference at the moment we are asked."3. Why don't they agree?"Partly each task has its own measurement error. Partly the tasks differ: for example, it helps explain the differences whether a riskless option is on offer."4. How are our two tasks different?"In the bomb box you choose one number. Each extra box raises the money and lowers the chance together. In the ten-row list the prizes stay the same, and only the chance changes from row to row."5. What does the bomb box pick up?"How far you go when more money means more danger. Opening 6 of 12 gives the most expected money: €18 with chance one half is €9. Opening 4 or 8 gives €8. It needs little maths."6. What does the Holt–Laury list pick up?"The row where you leave the safer lottery A for the riskier lottery B. By expected money, A is ahead in rows 1 to 4 and B from row 5 on."7. What if I switched back and forth?"Going one row down moves one number of the die to the high prize. In A that number gains €8, in B it gains €75. So once B is better for you, it should stay better (if more money is always better for you). If you went back, we can't read one attitude from your list. It also happens in real studies that use lists like this."· Row 10 is a check: €40 for sure or €77 for sure. Choosing A there was probably a slip.8. The money is imaginary. Does that matter?"It can. In Holt and Laury's list, with real money the average number of safe choices rose with the stakes: 6.0, 6.8 and 7.2 at 20, 50 and 90 times. With imaginary money it changed little: 4.9, 5.1 and 5.3. Our list has their dollar numbers times 20, in euros, and nobody is paid. So read our result as a picture of this room, not as a precise measurement."9. Does the order of the choices matter?"It is a fair worry. In that first study the big real-money choice always came after the small real one and the big imaginary one, and other researchers criticised this. Holt and Laury ran it again with each person doing only one version. Higher real stakes still led to more safe choices, 5.7 against 6.7. Higher imaginary stakes hardly changed anything, 5.6 against 5.7."10.
