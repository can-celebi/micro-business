Choice under uncertainty is choice over gambles.
A job: take the offer now, or wait for a better one?
A flat: sign now, or keep looking?
A flight: buy now, or hope it gets cheaper?
A gamble is a list of outcomes, each with a chance.

John von Neumann

Born in Budapest in 1903; died in Washington in 1957.
1928: the minimax theorem for games.
1933: professor at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, with Einstein.
He also gave quantum mechanics its mathematics and was one of the pioneers of computer science.
1944: Theory of Games and Economic Behavior, with Oskar Morgenstern. The proof of expected utility came in the 2nd edition, 1947.

motivation

If our preferences follow four conditions (they come next), every lottery has one number: its expected utility.
The shape of u will tell us how a person treats risk.
The lottery with the higher expected utility is the one you prefer.

expected utility

Expected utility (EU): multiply the utility of each outcome by its probability and add up.
Written out: EU = p₁ · u(x₁) + p₂ · u(x₂) + … + pₙ · u(xₙ).
Shorter: EU = Σ pᵢ · u(xᵢ).
Σ is a capital Greek S, for 'sum': add up over all outcomes.
Read it as: for every outcome, multiply its probability by its utility; then add everything up.
Lottery 1 has two outcomes, so two terms: 0.4 · 0.6 + 0.6 · 0.
In the same way, EV = Σ pᵢ · xᵢ.

expected utility with numbers

Wealth €400, U(M) = √M. A coin bet: win €129 or lose €39.
After the bet, wealth is €529 or €361.
The utilities are √529 = 23 and √361 = 19.
EU = ½ · 23 + ½ · 19 = 21.

A common slip: ½ · 529 + ½ · 361 = 445 is expected wealth, not expected utility.

risk attitudes

Compare the gamble with its expected wealth for sure.
Risk averse: you prefer the expected wealth for sure, for every risky gamble. That is a concave U.
Risk neutral: you are indifferent. That is a linear U.
Risk seeking: you prefer the gamble. That is a convex U.
