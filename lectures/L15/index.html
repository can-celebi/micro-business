completeness: a question

Three friends look at the same two lottery tickets: ticket 1 = (best 50%, worst 50%), ticket 2 = X for sure. Each friend has their own taste.
Pia: “Ticket 2.”
Tim: “I like them exactly the same.”
Cem: “I have no ranking for these two: not 1, not 2, not ‘the same’.”
Whose answer breaks completeness?
answer
Cem
Completeness asks for one of three answers: prefer 1, prefer 2, or indifferent. “Exactly the same” is an answer; only Cem gives none.

transitivity: a question

Dana prefers ticket A to ticket B, B to C, and C to A. Dana holds ticket C.
A dealer says: “Give me €1 and your ticket, and I'll give you a ticket you prefer to it.” Dana accepts every offer.
After three swaps, what does Dana hold, and how much has Dana paid?
answer
ticket C again, €3 paid
C → B (€1), B → A (€1), A → C (€1): back at C, €3 poorer, and the dealer can go on forever.
With expected utility this cannot happen: every ticket gets one number, and numbers cannot go round in a circle.

continuity: a question

Best ≻ X ≻ worst. Lea says: “If there is any chance at all of the worst, even 1 in a million, I take X for sure. Only the best for sure beats X.”
Is there a chance p that makes Lea indifferent between X for sure and the lottery ticket (best p, worst 1 − p)?
answer
No: continuity fails for Lea.
For every p below 1, Lea takes X; at p = 1 the lottery ticket is the best for sure and Lea takes it. No p gives a tie.
Murat Hoca 23_leonardo/frozen/a9a93a9a90e6 (Ulutin passed). Poll 3.

independence: a question

You prefer X for sure to the lottery ticket T = (best 50%, worst 50%).
Now each comes in a box: with chance 50% you get what is in it, and with chance 50% you get a cinema voucher V instead.
Box 1 = (X 50%, V 50%) · Box 2 = (T 50%, V 50%)
If your preferences satisfy independence, which box do you choose?
answer
Box 1
The voucher half is the same in both boxes, so only the other half decides, and there you prefer X to T.
With expected utility: 0.5 · 0.6 + 0.5 · u(V) > 0.5 · 0.5 + 0.5 · u(V) for every u(V). The voucher term cancels.

utility functions and risk preferences

Remember utility functions over goods, and their indifference curves.
Different functional forms represented different preferences over goods.
The same holds here: utility functions with different shapes represent different preferences over lotteries.
We call these preferences our risk preferences.
Can, Additions 53, 56: goods' graphs, then the same for lotteries; no 'next'.

utility of money

So far the outcomes were things: a smartphone, headphones, a coffee.
Now we think simply in terms of money, with which we can buy things.
Let M be our wealth, in euros.
On the horizontal axis: money M. On the vertical axis: the utility we get from that money, U(M).
We look at three types of curves, one at a time.
Can, Addition 45.

a linear utility function

A function is linear if its graph is a straight line.
Equal steps in wealth add equal steps in utility.
Constant marginal utility: each extra euro adds the same.
An example: U(M) = M.
Can, Additions 45, 48, 56: type first, the formula last.

decreasing marginal utility: a concave function

A function is concave if the straight line between any two points of its graph lies below the graph (or on it).
Equal steps in wealth add less and less utility.
Decreasing marginal utility: each extra euro adds less than the one before.
An example: U(M) = √M.
Can, Additions 45, 48, 56: type first, the formula last.

increasing marginal utility: a convex function

A function is convex if the straight line between any two points of its graph lies above the graph (or on it).
Equal steps in wealth add more and more utility.
Increasing marginal utility: each extra euro adds more than the one before.
An example: U(M) = M².
Can, Additions 45, 48, 56: type first, the formula last.

common concave utility functions: CRRA and CARA

Two families of concave utility functions have names: CRRA (constant relative risk aversion) and CARA (constant absolute risk aversion).
With CRRA, the share of wealth you would risk stays the same as you get richer; with CARA, the euro amount stays the same.
CRRA: U(M) = M1−r/(1−r), and U(M) = ln M for r = 1; our √M is r = ½. · CARA: U(M) = 1 − e−aM, a > 0.
Arif (Wikipedia 'Risk aversion'; Ulutin passed); Celebi: one slide, two graphs, plain sentence, formulas small and last. Example if asked: CRRA √M: 50–50 of 16 and 64 has risk premium 4; times 4 (64 or 256) gives 16. CARA: add the same amount to every outcome and the risk premium stays the same.

expected utility of a lottery

Remember lottery 1: H with chance 40%, C with chance 60%.
lottery 1
H with chance 40%
C with chance 60%
Expected utility: multiply the utility of each outcome by its chance, and add up.
For lottery 1: EU = 0.4 · u(H) + 0.6 · u(C).
With u(H) = 0.6 and u(C) = 0: EU = 0.4 · 0.6 + 0.6 · 0 = 0.24.
With money and a linear utility u(x) = x, the expected utility is the same as the expected value: EU = EV.
Can, Addition 56: remind the lottery, then the formula step by step, then compare with EV.

a gamble with money

Your wealth is €1,600.
A gamble: with a 50% chance you win €4,800; with a 50% chance you lose €1,500.
If you win, your wealth is 1,600 + 4,800 = €6,400.
If you lose, your wealth is 1,600 − 1,500 = €100.
Can, Additions 47, 54: never 'a coin bet'; 'a 50% chance'.

its expected utility

In symbols: EU = ½ · U(6,400) + ½ · U(100).
Now take U(M) = √M: EU = ½ · √6,400 + ½ · √100.
With the numbers: EU = ½ · 80 + ½ · 10 = 45.
Without the gamble you keep €1,600: U(1,600) = √1,600 = 40.
45 > 40, so this person takes the gamble.
Can, Addition 56: U(100) and U(6,400) first, then √, then the numbers.

expected wealth and its utility

Your wealth is €1,600 and your utility is U(M) = √M.
With a 50% chance you win €4,800; with a 50% chance you lose €1,500.
If you win: 1,600 + 4,800 = €6,400, so U = √6,400 = 80.
If you lose: 1,600 − 1,500 = €100, so U = √100 = 10.
EU = ½ · 80 + ½ · 10 = 45.
Without the gamble: √1,600 = 40. Since 45 > 40, the person takes the gamble.
The gamble's expected wealth is ½ · 6,400 + ½ · 100 = €3,250.
If you had €3,250 for sure, your utility would be √3,250 ≈ 57.
The gamble, with the same expected wealth, gives an expected utility of only 45.
57 > 45: this person prefers the expected wealth for sure to the gamble. The gap, measured in utility, is what risk costs them. Tomorrow we call this risk aversion.

risk averse, risk neutral, risk seeking

Risk averse: for every risky gamble, U(expected wealth) > EU. The curve is concave, like √M.
Risk neutral: for every risky gamble, U(expected wealth) = EU. The curve is linear.
Risk seeking: for every risky gamble, U(expected wealth) < EU. The curve is convex.
Can, Addition 56.

the certainty equivalent

The certainty equivalent (CE) is the amount of money for sure that gives the same utility as the gamble: U(CE) = EU.
Here: √CE = 45.
So CE = 45² = €2,025.
This person is indifferent between the gamble and €2,025 for sure.
Can, Addition 56; numbers Celebi/Ulutin (25.11).

the risk premium

The risk premium is the expected wealth minus the certainty equivalent: risk premium = expected wealth − CE.
Here: 3,250 − 2,025 = €1,225.
The risk premium is the expected money this person gives up to avoid the risk.
Risk averse: risk premium > 0. Risk neutral: risk premium = 0. Risk seeking: risk premium < 0.
Can, Addition 56.

certainty equivalent and risk premium: an exercise

A person has no other wealth and holds a lottery ticket that pays €3,844 or €676, each with chance ½. U(M) = √M.
What is the certainty equivalent of the ticket?
answer
€1,936
Expected utility: ½ · √3,844 + ½ · √676 = ½ · 62 + ½ · 26 = 44.
Certainty equivalent: √CE = 44, so CE = 44² = €1,936.
Expected value: ½ · 3,844 + ½ · 676 = €2,260. Risk premium: 2,260 − 1,936 = €324.
Would the person sell the ticket for €2,000? Yes: 2,000 > 1,936.
