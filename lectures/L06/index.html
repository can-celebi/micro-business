Everything you can just afford with your budget at today's prices:px x  +  py y  =  B
ends

slope
−pxpy: the relative price, what one more x costs in units of y
income up

price of x up
the line rotates in; the end on the y-axis stays
composite good

preferences

A preference ranks bundles (x, y): for any two bundles it says which one you like at least as much.
A ≿ B
A is at least as good as B
A ≻ B
A is strictly better: A ≿ B, but not B ≿ A
A ∼ B
indifferent: A ≿ B and B ≿ A
example
A = (10, €30) and B = (8, €40) (coffees, euros): which one would you pick?

completeness

Any two bundles can be compared: A ≿ B, or B ≿ A, or both.
means
you can always say which one you like at least as much
not the same
"I can't decide" (no ranking) is not "I'm indifferent" (equally good)

transitivity

If A ≿ B and B ≿ C, then A ≿ C: no cycles in your ranking.
first
A ≻ B
then
B ≻ C
so
A ≻ C: that is transitivity
a cycle
if instead C ≻ A: there is no best bundle

the money pump

Someone with a cycle in their preferences can be made to pay again and again, and end where they started.
the cycle
A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A
swap 1
you hold C; pay €1 to move to B (you prefer B)
swap 2
pay €1 to move to A
swap 3
pay €1 to move to C (you prefer C to A)
after one loop
you hold C again and are €3 poorer; repeat for ever

where cycles come from

Real people can end up with cycles, for example when they judge on several things at once.
price
ranks the options A ≻ B ≻ C
quality
ranks them B ≻ C ≻ A
speed
ranks them C ≻ A ≻ B
A beats B
on price and speed (2 of 3)
B beats C
on price and quality (2 of 3)
C beats A
on quality and speed (2 of 3): a cycle, though each single ranking is fine

more is better

More of a good, and no less of the other, is strictly better (both goods are goods, not bads).
more coffee
(12, 30) ≻ (10, 30): 2 more coffees, same euros
more euros
(10, 40) ≻ (10, 30): same coffees, €10 more
all better
everything up and to the right of (10, 30): more of one, no less of the other
less is worse
(8, 30) and (10, 20) are worse, and so is everything down and to the left
careful
it needs goods; for a bad (spam, noise) more is worse

bundles as good as A

Find all the bundles that are exactly as good as one bundle A, one at a time.
start
take one bundle: A = (12, 40)
worse and better
W = (2, 30) has less of both: worse; P = (20, 100) has more of both: better
in between
walk along the straight line from W to P: somewhere you pass a bundle exactly as good as A (if tastes have no sudden jumps, which we assume)
again
other worse and better pairs give more bundles as good as A
join them
the curve through all of them is the indifference curve through A

a map of curves, like altitude

Think of how much you like a bundle as height: every bundle has a height, and better bundles are higher.
the hill
satisfaction as a hill over the floor of bundles (x, y)
slices
cut the hill at three heights
from above
look down: three curves, u1 < u2 < u3, like contour lines on a hiking map
only the order
we never need the heights, only which curve is higher

convexity

Averages are at least as good as extremes: if A ∼ B, then the half-half mix of A and B is at least as good as A.
the mix
A = (20, 0), B = (0, 20): the mix is (20 + 02, 0 + 202) = (10, 10)
intuition
a week of only pizza or only salad vs. some of each: most people like some balance
on the graph
the straight line between A and B lies on or above the curve: the curve bows toward the origin
only weakly
if the mix is exactly as good (never better), the curve is a straight line: still convex, just not strictly

indifference curves

An indifference curve shows all bundles you like exactly as much as one bundle A.
start
take one bundle: A = (12, 40), 12 coffees and €40
better
everything up and to the right of A has more of both: better than A
worse
everything down and to the left has less of both: worse than A
the curve
so the bundles as good as A lie up-left and down-right of A: the curve slopes down
the map
every bundle has its own curve; curves further out are better

try it: better, worse, as good

Drag the bundle A: up-right is better, down-left is worse, the curve through A is as good.

properties of indifference curves

Each property comes from one rule.
slope down
more is better
never cross
transitivity together with more is better
one through every bundle
completeness
bowed toward the origin
convexity

why curves never cross

Suppose two indifference curves did cross, and follow the logic one step at a time.
suppose
two curves cross at A
on curve 1
B is on curve 1, so B ∼ A
on curve 2
C is on curve 2, so A ∼ C
transitivity
so B ∼ C
more is better
but C has more of both than B, so C ≻ B
contradiction
both cannot be true: curves never cross

different tastes, different maps

The rules are the same for everyone; the shape of the curves is personal.
coffee lover
gives up a lot of euros for one more coffee: steep curves
cares little
gives up only a few euros: flat curves
same bundle
at the same bundle (10, 10), the coffee lover would give up far more euros for one more coffee

the marginal rate of substitution

The MRS is how many units of y you would give up for one more unit of x, and stay exactly as well off.
one step
(1, 25) → (4, 16): Δx = +3 coffees, Δy = −9 euros, and you are just as happy
rise over run
slope ΔyΔx = −93 = −3: €3 less for every extra coffee
per coffee
over this step: −ΔyΔx = 3 euros per coffee; the MRS at one point comes from shrinking the step (next slide)

zoom in: the slope at one point

Make the step smaller and smaller: the trade-off per coffee settles at one number, the slope of the curve at that point.
7 more
(9, 9) → (16, 4): Δx = 7, Δy = −5, 0.71 per coffee
1 more
(9, 9) → (10, 8.05): Δx = 1, Δy = −0.95, 0.95 per coffee
0.1 more
Δx = 0.1, Δy = −0.099, 0.99 per coffee: it settles at 1
the tangent line
the straight line that touches the curve at (9, 9) has slope −1; so the MRS at (9, 9) is 1
tomorrow
with a utility function, the same number from partial derivatives, holding utility constant

try it: zoom in

Zoom in on the curve until it looks straight; then take small and big steps along the curve.

the MRS falls along the curve

Take the same kind of step further along the curve and the trade-off gets smaller.
(4, 16) → (9, 9)
Δx = +5, Δy = −7: 1.4 per coffee
(9, 9) → (16, 4)
Δx = +7, Δy = −5: 0.71 per coffee
(16, 4) → (25, 1)
Δx = +9, Δy = −3: 0.33 per coffee
at each point
these are averages over a step; the MRS at the points themselves (the tangent slopes): 5 at (1, 25), 2 at (4, 16), 1 at (9, 9), 0.5 at (16, 4), 0.2 at (25, 1)
diminishing
your willingness to pay for one more coffee (in euros of everything else) falls the more coffees you already have: the curve bows in

your rate and the market's rate

Both are a slope, Δy over Δx: how much y goes for one more x.
your rate
along your indifference curve (left)
the market's rate
along the budget line (right)
not the same
the MRS is inside you; the price ratio is in the shop
your rate: the MRS
MRS = −ΔyΔx
the market's rate: the price ratio
−ΔyΔx = pxpy  (with py = 1: just px)

remember this one

MRS = pxpy
the most important equation of the course
at the best affordable bundle (unless it sits at a corner), your rate (the slope of your indifference curve) equals the market's rate (the slope of the budget line). Tomorrow we find that bundle.

bowed out: not convex

Some people dislike mixing: then the average is worse than the extremes.
example
red or blue paint for your room, never purple: A ∼ B, but the half-half mix is worse
on the graph
the curve bows away from the origin: the mix lies below it

perfect substitutes

Two goods that do the same job at a fixed rate: you always give up the same amount of one for one more of the other.
example
50-cent coins and €1 coins: 2 of one always replace 1 of the other
curves
straight lines
MRS
constant: the same at every bundle
convex?
weakly: the average of two equally good bundles is exactly as good

perfect complements

Two goods you only use together in fixed proportions.
example
sausages and buns: one of each makes a hot dog
curves
L-shapes, with the kink where the proportions are right
extra buns
(3, 5): 3 sausages, 5 buns: still 3 hot dogs
extra sausages
(5, 3): still 3 hot dogs; more of both is what helps
MRS
0 on the flat arm, very large on the steep arm

corners

Remember perfect substitutes (the 50-cent and €1 coins): their indifference curves are straight lines. Then the best affordable bundle is usually an end of the budget line.
compare

MRS > pxpy

MRS < pxpy
spend everything on y
bowed out too

tomorrow

utility: ordinal, not cardinal

A utility function puts a number on each bundle, so that a better bundle gets a higher number.

revealed preference

If you chose A when B was also affordable, you have revealed that you like A at least as much as B.
what we see
the budget line and your choice on it, nothing else
a contradiction
on line 1 you choose A while B is affordable; on line 2 you choose B while A is affordable

two lines: consistent or not?

It only depends on where the two picks sit: on the inner piece of a line (below the other line) or on its outer piece.
the lines
line a: ends (20, 20) and line b: ends (12, 24); they cross at (4, 16)
inner pieces
shaded: a bundle there is affordable on the other line
both inner
A = (2, 18) and B = (8, 8): each was affordable when the other was picked: a contradiction
both outer
(10, 10) and (2, 20): neither was affordable on the other line: consistent
one of each
only one pick is revealed preferred to the other: consistent

three lines: a cycle

Revealed preferences can be chained: A over B and B over C means A over C.
line 1
ends (20, 60): you pick A = (10, 30); B was affordable, so A over B; C was not
line 2
ends (100, 20): you pick B = (10, 18); C was affordable, so B over C; A was not
A and B alone
A was not affordable on line 2: only A over B, no contradiction yet (both just beat the crossing point K)
line 3
ends (40, 80): you pick C = (35, 10); A and B were both affordable, so C over A and C over B
direct clash
B over C (line 2) and C over B (line 3)
through the chain
A over C via B, and C over A; B over A via C, and A over B
every pair
in the cycle A → B → C → A every pair contradicts: one directly, two through the chain

the test: GARP

Put all the revealed preferences together and look for cycles.
GARP
no cycle in your revealed preferences (with at least one strict step)
Afriat
no cycle ⇔ some well-behaved preferences (complete, transitive, more is better, convex) explain all your choices
so
checking for cycles is enough: no formula for preferences needed

the same line twice

Two different answers on the same budget line are a tie, not a contradiction.
first time
you pick A on the line
second time
the same line again: you pick B
both cost the whole budget
A at least as good as B, and B at least as good as A: A ∼ B
GARP
forbids only cycles with a strict step (a bundle strictly cheaper than the budget): none here
a stricter test
if the best bundle must be unique (strictly convex), two answers clash (the strong axiom, SARP)

how close to consistent? the CCEI

Shrink every budget line a little and ask: when does the contradiction disappear?
the clash
A = (2, 18) on line a, B = (8, 8) on line b: each was affordable when the other was picked
A was cheaper
on line b, A cost only 91.7% of the budget, yet you paid 100% for B
shrink to 95%
line b moves in: A is still inside, still a clash
shrink to 91.7%
the shrunk line runs through A; a bit more and the clash is gone
CCEI ≈ 0.92
consistent once we forgive wasting about 8% of a budget; 1 = fully consistent
