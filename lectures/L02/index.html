perfect competition

the benchmark market
A market is perfectly competitive when all five conditions hold.
1
many small buyers and sellers (fragmented industry)
2
price takers: no one can move the price
3
identical products: buyers don't care whom they buy from
4
perfect information: everyone knows prices and quality
5
free entry: anyone can start selling
Price taker holds for buyers too, and for firms buying their inputs (PS2 Q3).

what is bitcoin mining?

computers compete to write the next page of bitcoin's public ledger, and get paid for it
the ledger
every bitcoin payment is written into one public list; a new page (a block) is added about every 10 minutes
the competition
special machines (ASIC chips) guess numbers billions of times a second; the first correct guess wins and writes the page
the reward
the winner gets 3.125 new bitcoin + the fees that senders attached to their payments
One simple slide (your G3 comment), so students can follow the mining examples. Costs (machines, electricity) come on the barriers slide; the fees come back on L02-12b (who gets into the next block?). Facts: 00_admin/crypto-facts_W02.md.

the competition

try it on your phone

the page
the payments + the previous page's fingerprint + one number you choose
scramble it
a fixed formula (SHA-256) turns the page into a code, the hash
the rule
the page only counts if the hash starts with many zeros
the catch
no shortcut: change the number, scramble, check, again and again
Students press [try the next number] a few times (hopeless by hand), then [let my computer guess]: a phone finds 4 zeros in a few seconds (~65,000 tries on average). First to find it raises a hand. Real bitcoin needs ~19 zeros in this notation; all miners together make roughly 10^21 guesses per second. Point: winning = making more guesses = more machines + more electricity. The previous page's fingerprint is inside every page, so changing an old page would mean redoing all the work after it (that's the 'chain'). Correction to a common picture: miners don't make the text 'fit' the next block; they search for the number that makes the hash small.

barriers to entry

what keeps new sellers out
A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for a new seller to enter a market.
cheap electricity
miners pay ~€0.05 per kWh in Texas or Paraguay; you pay ~€0.25–0.30 in Vienna
capital
a professional bitcoin mine runs thousands of machines (ASIC chips) at ~€1,000–4,000 each
know-how and scale
cooling, buildings, contracts with power plants
better machines
every new generation makes older machines unprofitable
In Frank's terms (Okan): cheap power earns a rent for its owner; the marginal miner earns zero profit. Sources and dates: 00_admin/crypto-facts_W02.md.

the setup
how the GPUs are set up to run a model decides how fast and how cheap each answer is: hosts compete on this
the lab

law of demand

price ↑ → quantity ↓
When a good's price rises, people buy less of it, all else equal.
demand form
price ↑ → quantity demanded ↓ (all else equal)
inverse form
the marginal willingness to pay decreases as the quantity increases · e.g. you would pay €20 for your first AI subscription, but very little for a second one
why
each extra unit is worth less to you than the one before
the picture
a downward-sloping (inverse) demand curve
'All else equal' prepares Thursday: when something else changes, the whole curve moves.

supply

price ↑ → more is offered
Supply: what quantity do sellers want to sell at a given price? It slopes up because each extra unit costs more to provide.
any firm
producing more needs overtime, extra staff, new machines: each extra unit costs more (rising marginal cost)
bitcoin miners
miners SELL bitcoin: they are paid in it. Bitcoin price low → the reward barely pays the electricity: only the most efficient machines run. Price high → even old, power-hungry machines pay off and are switched back on
used machines
the higher the resale price, the more miners sell their old machine instead of running it
Rising marginal cost: each extra unit comes from a less efficient machine / an owner who values the machine more. Mirror of the demand slide.
