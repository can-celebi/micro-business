indifference curves

An indifference curve shows all bundles you like exactly as much as one bundle A.
start
take one bundle: A = (12, 40), 12 coffees and €40
better
everything up and to the right of A has more of both: better than A
worse
everything down and to the left has less of both: worse than A
the curve
so the bundles as good as A lie up-left and down-right of A: the curve slopes down
the map
every bundle has its own curve; curves further out are better

properties of indifference curves

Each property comes from one rule.
slope down
more is better
never cross
transitivity together with more is better
one through every bundle
completeness
bowed toward the origin
convexity

the marginal rate of substitution

The MRS is how many units of y you would give up for one more unit of x, and stay exactly as well off.
one step
(1, 25) → (4, 16): Δx = +3 coffees, Δy = −9 euros, and you are just as happy
rise over run
slope ΔyΔx = −93 = −3: €3 less for every extra coffee
per coffee
over this step: −ΔyΔx = 3 euros per coffee; the MRS at one point comes from shrinking the step (next slide)

zoom in: the slope at one point

Make the step smaller and smaller: the trade-off per coffee settles at one number, the slope of the curve at that point.
7 more
(9, 9) → (16, 4): Δx = 7, Δy = −5, 0.71 per coffee
1 more
(9, 9) → (10, 8.05): Δx = 1, Δy = −0.95, 0.95 per coffee
0.1 more
Δx = 0.1, Δy = −0.099, 0.99 per coffee: it settles at 1
the tangent line
the straight line that touches the curve at (9, 9) has slope −1; so the MRS at (9, 9) is 1
tomorrow
with a utility function, the same number from partial derivatives, holding utility constant

try it: zoom in

Zoom in on the curve until it looks straight; then take small and big steps along the curve.

the MRS falls along the curve

Take the same kind of step further along the curve and the trade-off gets smaller.
(4, 16) → (9, 9)
Δx = +5, Δy = −7: 1.4 per coffee
(9, 9) → (16, 4)
Δx = +7, Δy = −5: 0.71 per coffee
(16, 4) → (25, 1)
Δx = +9, Δy = −3: 0.33 per coffee
at each point
these are averages over a step; the MRS at the points themselves (the tangent slopes): 5 at (1, 25), 2 at (4, 16), 1 at (9, 9), 0.5 at (16, 4), 0.2 at (25, 1)
diminishing
your willingness to pay for one more coffee (in euros of everything else) falls the more coffees you already have: the curve bows in

your rate and the market's rate

Both are a slope, Δy over Δx: how much y goes for one more x.
your rate
along your indifference curve (left)
the market's rate
along the budget line (right)
not the same
the MRS is inside you; the price ratio is in the shop
your rate: the MRS
MRS = −ΔyΔx
the market's rate: the price ratio
−ΔyΔx = pxpy  (with py = 1: just px)

five cases: same bundle, different people

Two people can stand at the same bundle and still trade off very differently. Their indifference curves through that bundle have different slopes: a different MRS.
the bundle
in every case: (10, 10)
the question
how much y would you give up for one more x? That is the MRS.
steep
a steep curve: you give up a lot of y for one more x (high MRS)
flat
a flat curve: you give up little y (low MRS)

case 1 · time

x = € today, y = € in one year. How much of next year's money would you give up for €1 more today?
patient
“I give up €1 next year for €1 more today.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 1
impatient
“I give up €2 next year for €1 more today.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 2
Same bundle, different curves: the impatient person's curve is steeper, so the MRS is higher (2 instead of 1).
Same bundle (10, 10) for both types. The dashed line is the slope at (10, 10): how much y the person gives up for one more x. Type 1 first, then type 2, then compare.

case 2 · sharing

x = pizza slices for me, y = pizza slices for my flatmate. How many of my flatmate's slices would I give up for one more slice for me?
altruistic
“At (10, 10), one more slice for me is worth one slice for my flatmate.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 1
selfish
“For one more slice for me, I'd let my flatmate have two fewer.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 2
Same bundle: the selfish curve is steeper (MRS 2) than the altruistic one (MRS 1).
Same bundle (10, 10) for both types. The dashed line is the slope at (10, 10): how much y the person gives up for one more x. Type 1 first, then type 2, then compare.

case 3 · giving

x = € given to a local charity, y = € given to a charity abroad.
balanced
“At (10, 10), €1 more locally is worth €1 abroad to me.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 1
local first
“€1 more locally is worth €2 abroad to me.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 2
Same bundle: the local-first giver's curve is steeper (MRS 2) than the balanced giver's (MRS 1).
Same bundle (10, 10) for both types. The dashed line is the slope at (10, 10): how much y the person gives up for one more x. Type 1 first, then type 2, then compare.

case 4 · sharing help between two groups

x = vaccine doses for a group in the US, y = doses for a group in China.
unbiased
“At (10, 10), one more dose for the US group is worth one dose for the China group to me.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 1
biased
“One more dose for the US group is worth two doses for the China group to me.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 2
Same bundle: the unbiased curve has MRS 1; a bias shows up as a steeper curve (MRS 2).
A bias toward the other group would make the curve flatter: MRS ½. The point is the slope, not who is favoured.
Same bundle (10, 10) for both types. The dashed line is the slope at (10, 10): how much y the person gives up for one more x. Type 1 first, then type 2, then compare.

case 5 · work and leisure

x = hours of leisure, y = income in €10s. How much income would you give up for one more hour off?
leisure lover
“I give up €20 of income for one more hour off.”
MRS at (10, 10) = 2
workaholic
“I give up only €5 of income for one more hour off.”
MRS at (10, 10) = ½
Same bundle: the leisure lover's MRS is 2 (€20 per hour), the workaholic's is ½ (€5 per hour).
Same bundle (10, 10) for both types. The dashed line is the slope at (10, 10): how much y the person gives up for one more x. Type 1 first, then type 2, then compare.

the best feasible bundle

Feasible: on or below the budget line. Best: on the highest indifference curve you can still reach. Prices: px = 3, py = 1, budget €60.
u₁
affordable, but it cuts the budget line: moving along the line reaches a better curve
u₃
better, but out of reach: no point of u₃ is affordable
u₂
the highest curve that still touches the line: the best feasible bundle (10, 30)
If the curves never get as flat or as steep as the line, the best bundle is a corner (more on Thursday).
Budget 3x + y = 60. Three curves u₁ < u₂ < u₃. u₂ touches the line at (10, 30): MRS there = 30/10 = 3 = price ratio. u₁ crosses the line at about (2.9, 51.2) and (17.1, 8.8). Bang for the buck in MRS words: MRS > px/py means x is cheap for you: buy more x.

the best feasible bundle: why there

MRS=pxpy=3
there
the curve and the line have the same slope: the line above
bang for the buck
if your MRS is above the price ratio, one more x is worth more y to you than it costs: buy more x. Below it: buy less x. Stop where they are equal.
ordinal
we only used which curve is higher, never by how much: the order is enough

remember this one

MRS = pxpy
the most important equation of the course

Two special shapes, with everyday goods.

two pens of the same kind; 50-cent and €1 coins

left and right gloves

perfect substitutes

Two goods that do the same job at a fixed rate: you always give up the same amount of one for one more of the other.
example
50-cent coins and €1 coins: 2 of one always replace 1 of the other
curves
straight lines
MRS
constant: the same at every bundle
convex?
weakly: the average of two equally good bundles is exactly as good

perfect complements

Two goods you only use together in fixed proportions.
example
sausages and buns: one of each makes a hot dog
curves
L-shapes, with the kink where the proportions are right
extra buns
(3, 5): 3 sausages, 5 buns: still 3 hot dogs
extra sausages
(5, 3): still 3 hot dogs; more of both is what helps
MRS
0 on the flat arm, very large on the steep arm

utility: ordinal, not cardinal

A utility function puts a number on each bundle, so that a better bundle gets a higher number.
numbers

is C three times as good as A? the answer is 3, 1.6 or 9, depending on the labels

utility functions

A utility function gives every bundle a number, so that a better bundle gets a higher number and equally good bundles get the same number.
curves
an indifference curve is all bundles with the same number: u(x, y) = 10, u(x, y) = 20, …
ordinal
only the order of the numbers matters, as on the best-bundle slide
today
the formulas behind the shapes we have seen: straight lines, L-shapes, smooth curves

utility functions · perfect substitutes

u(x,y)=a⁢x+b⁢y
perfect substitutes
example
two brands of bottled water: u = x + y (a litre is a litre)
curves
straight lines: x + y = 10, = 20, …
MRS
the same at every bundle: ab, here 1
a x + b y: one unit of x replaces a over b units of y, everywhere.

utility functions · perfect complements

u(x,y)=min⁡{a⁢x,b⁢y}
perfect complements
example
sausages and buns: u = min{x, y} hot dogs
curves
L-shapes with the corner where a x = b y
why min
the smaller of the two decides: 10 sausages and 15 buns still make 10 hot dogs
min{a x, b y}: the corner is where the mix is right; extra of one good alone adds nothing.

utility functions · Cobb-Douglas

u(x,y)=xα⁢yβ
Cobb-Douglas
curves
smooth and bowed in: the MRS falls as you move down the curve
our cases
patient, altruistic, balanced, unbiased: x y · impatient, selfish, local first, biased, leisure lover: x² y · workaholic: x y²
α and β
a bigger α makes x matter more: a steeper curve through the same bundle
Curves through (10, 10): xy = 100 (MRS 1), x²y = 1000 (MRS 2), xy² = 1000 (MRS ½). The MRS formula comes on Thursday.

utility functions · a mixture

u(x,y)=α⁢x+yβ
with 0<β<1: linear in one good, curved in the other
example
u=2⁢x+y
in x
every unit of x adds the same, as with perfect substitutes
in y
each extra unit of y adds a little less
curves
bowed in, and each is the same curve shifted sideways: at a given y all curves have the same slope. They reach the x-axis, so corners are possible.
u = 2x + √y (Fatih, Murat PASS): the curves are y = (u − 2x)² for u = 8, 10, 12; they meet the x-axis at x = 4, 5, 6, each the previous curve shifted 1 to the right. MRS = 4√y: 8 at (3, 4) and at (4, 4), 16 at (2, 16). The MRS is 4√y (Thursday: derivatives): it depends only on y, so the curves are horizontal shifts of each other. Linear in x, concave in y: a quasi-concave mixture.

revealed preference

If you chose A when B was also affordable, you have revealed that you like A at least as much as B.
what we see
the budget line and your choice on it, nothing else
a contradiction
on line 1 you choose A while B is affordable; on line 2 you choose B while A is affordable

two lines: consistent or not?

It only depends on where the two picks sit: on the inner piece of a line (below the other line) or on its outer piece.
the lines
line a: ends (20, 20) and line b: ends (12, 24); they cross at (4, 16)
inner pieces
shaded: a bundle there is affordable on the other line
both inner
A = (2, 18) and B = (8, 8): each was affordable when the other was picked: a contradiction
both outer
(10, 10) and (2, 20): neither was affordable on the other line: consistent
one of each
only one pick is revealed preferred to the other: consistent

three lines: a cycle

Revealed preferences can be chained: A over B and B over C means A over C.
line 1
ends (20, 60): you pick A = (10, 30); B was affordable, so A over B; C was not
line 2
ends (100, 20): you pick B = (10, 18); C was affordable, so B over C; A was not
A and B alone
A was not affordable on line 2: only A over B, no contradiction yet (both just beat the crossing point K)
line 3
ends (40, 80): you pick C = (35, 10); A and B were both affordable, so C over A and C over B
direct clash
B over C (line 2) and C over B (line 3)
through the chain
A over C via B, and C over A; B over A via C, and A over B
every pair
in the cycle A → B → C → A every pair contradicts: one directly, two through the chain

how close to consistent? the CCEI

what it stands for
CCEI = Critical Cost Efficiency Index (Afriat, 1972)
what it does
shrink every budget line by the same share e until the choices stop contradicting each other
what it shows
the CCEI is that share: 1 = fully consistent; 0.9 = as if just over 10 % of the money was burned
Precise version: the CCEI is the share where the contradictions stop; at every share below it, the choices could come from rational preferences for which more is better. The lower the CCEI, the further from rational. Example on the next slide (CCEI about 0.92). Generic numbers only, nothing from PS3.

the CCEI: an example

Shrink every budget line a little and ask: when does the contradiction disappear?
the clash
A = (2, 18) on line a, B = (8, 8) on line b: each was affordable when the other was picked
A was cheaper
on line b, A costs only 2224≈91.7% of line b's money, yet you spent all of it on B
shrink to 95%
line b moves in: A is still inside, still a clash
shrink to 91.7%
the shrunk line runs through A; a bit more and the clash is gone
CCEI ≈ 0.92
consistent once we forgive wasting about 8% of a budget; 1 = fully consistent

One chooser, 12 choices on 12 budget lines. Step through each contradiction, then shrink every budget until none is left.
